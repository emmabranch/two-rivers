Chapter 2 · Flood

Sumas Prairie, November 2021

Sumas Prairie is farmland on the bed of a lake. Semá:th X̱ó:tsa (Sumas Lake) was at the heart of Semá:th territory until it was drained between 1920 and 1924 to make farmland. In the Semá:th First Nation's words, the draining "brought profound and lasting harm to the Stó:lō people."

Read about Semá:th territory on the Sumas First Nation's website

Chapter 2 · Flood

Three storms in a month

On 14 November, an atmospheric river brought two days of heavy rain. The Nooksack River overflowed in Washington, and the water ran north across the border into Sumas Prairie. More than 3,300 people from 1,100 properties had to leave, and many farm animals were lost. Two more storms followed before the prairie could drain. Keep scrolling to watch each day.

Chapter 2 · Flood

After the water

For people: a flood doesn't end when the water goes down. BC's climate and health assessment found that the emotional impact of recent floods and wildfires lasts long after the immediate danger has passed.

For the climate: climate scientists found that human-caused climate change made atmospheric rivers like this one at least 60% more likely. And it happened again: in December 2025, the Nooksack overtopped its banks and water crossed into Sumas Prairie.

Sources: Climate Change and Health in BC: From Risk to Resilience, Chapter 9; Gillett et al. (2022), Human influence on the 2021 British Columbia floods; City of Abbotsford, Flood Response and Recovery.

Chapter 2 · Flood

And then, tulips

Recovery takes years. Lakeland Flowers, on Sumas Prairie, was flooded in 2021. In April 2023, the Abbotsford Tulip Festival came back, its first since 2019.

Source: Country Life in BC, 19 April 2023.
